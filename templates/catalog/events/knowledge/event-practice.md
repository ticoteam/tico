# Event practice

The standing method behind `playbooks/plan-an-event.md`, `playbooks/weekly-events-review.md` and `playbooks/onboarding.md`.
`calendar.md`, `rules.md` and `results.md` hold the team's own events, budgets and results. This file holds what is true
about events in general. Sources were read on 2026-11-01 through search excerpts unless marked otherwise.

## Judge events by meetings and pipeline

- Attendance is context. The measure of an event is meetings held and pipeline it leads to, against its full cost
  (practice; the same rule is in `knowledge/rules.md`).
- Cost per meeting is cost divided by meetings held. The baseline is the team's own last similar events. No dated public
  figure for trade-show cost per qualified meeting was found in this pass. The CEIR exhibitor ROI study and its benchmark
  playbook exist, but their figures were not read. Do not quote a vendor figure as the team's own result.

## Webinars

- Registration-to-attendance: ON24's 2026 report, using 2025 data, gives an average of 60 percent, up from 57 percent in
  2024. ON24 counts live and on-demand attendance together; about two-thirds of attendees joined live. Demio describes
  35 to 50 percent as a healthy attendance rate for most B2B webinars. GoToWebinar's 44 percent figure is about seven
  years old. All of these come from vendors using their own platform data, so they are not independent benchmarks.
- Practice: use the team's own rate from its last three webinars. Count meetings after the webinar, not sign-ups.

## Lead response

- Harvard Business Review, "The Short Life of Online Sales Leads" (March 2011), by Oldroyd, McElheran and Elkington. Of
  2,241 US firms tested with a web lead, 37 percent replied within an hour, 24 percent took more than 24 hours, and 23
  percent never replied. Firms that reached out within an hour were nearly seven times as likely to qualify the lead as
  firms that waited even an hour longer. The data is from 2011. One co-author is the chairman and CEO of a sales-tools
  company, which readers should weigh.
- Practice: set the follow-up rule in `knowledge/rules.md` (for example, within two business days) and count it on each
  event's results line.

## Consent and capture (UK, PECR and UK GDPR, ICO guidance)

- Corporate subscribers (companies and LLPs) are outside PECR's consent rule for email marketing, but the sender must
  give a valid address for opt-outs. The ICO's view is that a corporate opt-out should be honoured.
- Individuals, and sole traders or some partnerships, need consent or the soft opt-in. The soft opt-in needs a sale or
  negotiation, and an opt-out offered when the details were collected and in each message. A badge scan with no sale is
  unlikely to meet that test for an individual (inference from the ICO guidance).
- A named work address can still object under UK GDPR Article 21, even at a company. Keep a do-not-contact record for
  every objection; do not delete the address.
- The ICO's worked example for a conference organiser plans for an opt-out at sign-up, a check for earlier objections, and
  a clear explanation of why people are contacted. The ICO pages carry ages of one to five years, and the ICO notes that
  the Data Use and Access Act moved the direct marketing definition into PECR in August 2025. Verify before quoting.
- Recording (practice, not searched for this file): tell registrants at sign-up that a session is recorded and who can
  see the recording. Some US states require every party's consent to a recording. Check with counsel before recording a
  session with outside guests.

## Post-event review

- Practice: one line per event, with goal, total cost, meetings held, pipeline, cost per meeting, follow-up count inside
  the rule, one thing to repeat and one thing to change. A figure not yet measurable is written as such.
