# Research methods

Seed for the method choices in briefs. Each rule is labelled with its source. **Primary**: the page was read.
**Secondary**: a summary only; check the book or paper before quoting it. **Team rule**: ours, not a published standard.
Cite the source and year in any brief that relies on one.

## Pick the method by the question

| Question | Method | Limit to state in the brief |
|---|---|---|
| Why people do what they do, and what they tried last time | Interview on past behaviour | A few people; name the segment |
| Whether a person can finish a task | Usability round, think aloud | Five per round; three to four per distinct user group |
| How many people have a problem | Survey of existing users, or counts from records | Counts only those who answered; a count is not proof of need |
| What people compare you with | Public sources on competitors | Record in the market graph with the source and date |
| Whether a change works | A test in the product, run by a person | The result that would change the decision, written first |

## Interviews: ask about the past

- Ask about the last real time the problem happened, not about your idea. Ask what they did, then what happened next.
  Rob Fitzpatrick, *The Mom Test* (2013). **Secondary**: from summaries; read the book before quoting it.
- Avoid "would you". People predict what they would do poorly. Ask what they did last time.
- Listen more than you talk. The brief quotes what they said, not what the interviewer said.

## Usability rounds: five per round, several rounds

- Jakob Nielsen, "Why You Only Need to Test with 5 Users" (Nielsen Norman Group, 2000). **Primary**: test no more than
  five users per round, run many small rounds, and redesign between them. For distinct user groups, use three or four
  per group.
- The article's "about 85 percent of problems" figure comes from a model (Nielsen and Landauer, 1993) that assumes a
  typical problem-finding rate of about 31 percent. That rate is an average across the author's projects. Do not quote
  85 percent as a fact about one study.
- Faulkner (2003), a simulation study. **Secondary**: from a summary, which reports wide variation between five-person
  samples, with some missing a large share of problems. Check the paper before you quote it.
- State the limit in every brief: five people find the common problems and can miss the rest.

## Sources and patterns

- **Team rule**: a pattern needs three separate sources from different people. One source is an anecdote and two is
  emerging. We set this rule; it is not a published standard.
- Teresa Torres, *Continuous Discovery Habits* (2021). **Primary** for the description: the author's site
  (producttalk.org) calls it a product trio's guide to continuous discovery, built around an opportunity solution tree.
  The weekly touchpoint cadence appears only in **secondary** summaries; check the book before you cite it.

## Checks for a brief

- The method and its limit are named.
- The sample is named by segment and size.
- Each claim carries its source count, and each rule carries its source and year.
