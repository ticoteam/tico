# Derive the voice from the company's best existing copy

A voice written from adjectives ("friendly, professional, innovative") fits every company and helps no writer. This
method finds the voice the company already has, with proof, and needs the owner for one decision at the end.

## 1. Build two sets

**The best set: 10 to 20 pieces, chosen on evidence.** For each piece, write why it is in, with the number or the
source. Take them from at least three of these:

- Pieces customers answered, quoted back or praised. Search replies and reviews for the company's own phrases.
- The pages, emails and ads with the best result on record: the conversion or reply number, with its window and where
  it was read. A piece with no number on record is not "best performing".
- Things the founder wrote by hand: a launch post, a reply to an angry customer, an update to investors, a README.
- Support replies with the best ratings, or the ones the support owner points to.
- The pieces the owner named in the setup answers. These always go in.

**The not-us set: at least five pieces.** Copy that underperformed on record, templated or agency copy the owner
dislikes, and two or three pages from the strongest rivals. This set is the contrast. Without it, every pattern looks
like a trait.

**Customer words: at least ten verbatim quotes** with who said it and where, including negative ones about the category
and about the company. Paraphrases and theme labels do not count.

Thin material (fewer than five pieces in the best set): say so on the task. Derive from the founder's own writing and
the customer words, tag every trait `default`, and set a review date three months out instead of six.

## 2. Pull the sentences that carry each piece

From each best piece, copy the three to five sentences that do the work: a picture, a fact, a stance, a joke, a
promise. Copy them exactly. Then describe each on features you can see, not on feelings:

| Feature | What to record |
|---|---|
| Length | Words per sentence. Is a short one followed by a long one? |
| Person | we, you, I, or none |
| Verbs and adjectives | Which carries the sentence |
| Concreteness | A thing you can picture, a number, a name, a time |
| Stance | Does it take a side or rule something out |
| Humour | None, dry, playful, at whose cost |
| Bad news | Says it first, softens it, or hides it |
| Jargon | The customer's word, the industry's word, or the company's own |
| Formality | Contractions, greetings, sign-offs |
| Habits | Openers, closers, punctuation, lists, questions |

Do the same for the not-us set.

## 3. Find what the best set shares and the not-us set lacks

Count. A pattern is a candidate trait when it shows in at least half of the best set's pulled sentences and in few or
none of the not-us set's. A pattern found in both sets is the category's voice, not the company's: leave it out. Write
each candidate as an observation first ("says the bad news in the first five words"), not as an adjective.

Check the candidates against the customer words. A trait customers echo back in their own words is strong. A trait
that fights how customers talk about the company goes to the owner as a question.

## 4. Name three or four traits

For each trait:

- **We are**: a plain name, in the company's own words where a line supplies one.
- **But not**: the trait's bad version. Take it from the not-us set when you can.
- **Sounds like**: at least two real lines from the best set, with their sources. One must come from the company's own
  story or a customer.
- **Not like**: one real line from the not-us set or from current copy.
- **By**: `owner` (they said or picked it), `accepted` (you showed it and they kept it), or `default` (they have not
  seen it yet).

Then write the **rhythm** in two or three lines: usual sentence length, the patterns the best set shows (a picture then
a fact; short lines; questions or none), and how pieces open and close.

Place the voice on four scales as a cross-check and as shorthand for outside writers: funny to serious, formal to
casual, respectful to irreverent, enthusiastic to matter-of-fact. The traits and the scales must agree. Where current
copy disagrees with them, list those pieces: they are the first fixes.

## 5. Run the rival swap on the set

Would the strongest rival's copywriter accept the whole voice table as theirs? Judge the set, not single lines: a
short common line can be very much the company's. If the set passes for the rival, push one trait further using
something only this company has (a founder's story, a policy, a way of working), or ask the owner what only they
would say.

## 6. The voice test: the owner's one decision

Take two real messages the company sends: one happy (a welcome, a launch) and one hard (the top complaint). Write each
three ways, under 60 words each. The three differ in **approach**:

- A: the plainest and shortest.
- B: built on the owner's own story or a customer's words from the best set.
- C: the boldest trait pushed further.

For the hard message, the three must differ in what they do (own it and fix it; explain why it happens; turn it into
the next good thing), not only in tone.

Ask: "Which is us? You can mix." Give no recommendation. Fold the pick into the voice table. Any line the owner calls
"most us" goes in exactly as they said it. Two rounds with no pick: keep the derived traits, tag them `default`, and
move on. No reply at all: the same, and the guide ships on the defaults.

## 7. What never to do

- Do not write the owner's story for them or offer a past decision as a yes or no question.
- Do not reword a line the owner or a customer said. Fix capitals only.
- Do not let one story carry every example. No story appears in more than two pieces.
- Do not grade your own work generously. Quote the line that proves each trait, or cut the trait.
