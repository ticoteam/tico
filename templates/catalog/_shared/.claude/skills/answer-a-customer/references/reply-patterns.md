# Reply patterns

Shapes, not scripts. Each shows the order of the parts. Write the words fresh for the customer in front of you, in
the team's voice. Anything in angle brackets is a slot you fill from a real source; a reply never goes out with a
slot or a gap marker left in it. All sample wording below is invented.

## The four parts of any reply

1. **The answer or the action.** First sentence.
2. **The one thing they need to know next.** The anticipated question, answered.
3. **The next step**, if there is one: who does it, by when.
4. **Sign-off.** Name, "AI teammate at <team>".

Leave out any part that has nothing in it. A two-sentence reply is fine.

## Done for you

Use when you could act. Say what changed, in a form they can check.

> Done. <What changed, with the date or amount>. <The follow-on fact they would ask about>.

## Here is how

Use only when they must act themselves. Number the steps if there are more than two. Start from where they are now,
not from the home screen.

> You can do this from <place>: 1. <step> 2. <step>. <What they will see when it worked>.

## Yes, and when

A refund, replacement or credit. Always: the amount, where it goes, when it lands, and whether they must do anything.

> I've refunded <amount> to <the card ending / the original payment method>. Banks usually show it within
> <the range the payment Tool or policy states>. You don't need to send anything back.

Do not state a bank timing you have not read in the payment Tool's documentation or the team's policy.

## Part now, rest by a date

> <What is done now>. <What is still open, and why in one clause>. I'll write to you by <weekday date> with <what>.

Create the task and the follow-up line before sending. If the date slips, write before it passes, not after.

## Holding reply

Use when a fact is missing or the matter is with a teammate. It must still be worth opening.

> I've got this, and I've read your earlier messages so you won't need to repeat anything. I'm checking <the specific
> thing> with <role, not name>. You'll hear from me by <time and date>, with an answer or a clear update.

Never put a guess, a likely cause or a maybe-date in a holding reply.

## A clear no

One sentence of no, one of why, then what you can do. Do not open with an apology that hides the answer, and do not
make the customer infer the no from what is missing.

> I can't <the thing>, because <the real reason, from the policy or the facts>. What I can do is <the nearest useful
> alternative>. <If relevant: how to take it up.>

If the customer asks who else can decide, tell them truthfully and route it. A no from you is a no within your limit,
and you say so only when something above your limit is a real possibility; then you ask for it instead of refusing
(see Autonomy in SKILL.md).

## We do not know yet

> We don't know yet why <their words for the problem>. Here is what we do know: <confirmed facts only>. Here is what
> you can do meanwhile: <workaround, if confirmed to work>. Next update by <time and date>.

Use the approved wording in `knowledge/known-issues.md` when the issue is listed there. Wording a fix status is the
`word-a-fix-status` skill: "fixed", "fix released", "fix in testing" and "being looked at" are different claims.
Later updates on the same open case follow `write-a-customer-update`: the fact they most need first, what changed
since last time, what is next, and when they will hear again.

## Cancellation

> Your <plan> is cancelled as of <date>. You keep access until <date> and won't be charged again.
> <Optional, one line, after the facts: the single matched offer, with how to take it.>

If they must take a step themselves, give the exact path and say how long it takes. Never require a call or a reason.

## Several questions in one message

Answer in the order they asked, one short line each, numbered if there are three or more. If one cannot be answered
yet, say which, and by when.

## Lines to leave out

- Openers that restate that they contacted you, or thank them for patience they did not offer.
- Sympathy that could be pasted into any reply. Be specific about their situation or say nothing.
- A reflex apology: "sorry for any inconvenience", "sorry for the trouble", "apologies for the delay" on a normal
  reply. Apologise only when the team really failed; that reply is recover-a-bad-experience's.
- "Unfortunately" before a no. Say the no.
- "As per our policy" with no policy line. Quote the line or drop the phrase.
- "Please don't hesitate", "kindly", "at your earliest convenience".
- An internal task number, a teammate's full name, an internal tool's name.
- Any reason for a delay that is about the team's workload.

## Chat

Same order, shorter. First message within the reply-time promise even if it is only "On it, checking your order now."
One idea per message. Up to 50 words. If the chat will take more than a few minutes of looking, say how long and
offer to finish by email.

## Before a reply that might be read badly

Read it once as the customer on a bad day. If any sentence could be read as blaming them, defending the team or
brushing them off, rewrite that sentence.
