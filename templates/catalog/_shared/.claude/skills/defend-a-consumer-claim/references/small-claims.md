# Small claims: preparing the company as defendant

Small-claims court is built for people without lawyers: low limits, simple forms, a short hearing, a quick decision.
For a company it is usually the cheapest place to be sued, provided someone shows up prepared. Rules differ by state
and sometimes by county. Read the notice and the court's own self-help page first, and record what you read and when.

California rules are given as the worked example because they are detailed and public (read 2026-11-18 on county
court pages). Do not assume another state matches.

## Day one

1. **Read the notice.** Court and address, case number, hearing date and time, the plaintiff's name, the amount,
   and the plaintiff's short statement of why. Save a copy to the matter folder.
2. **Record service.** How and when the papers reached the company, and who took them. If service looks wrong (a
   stranger's address, far too late), write it down for the person appearing. Do not ignore the case on that basis.
3. **Check who is named.** If the papers name the wrong entity or misspell it, the company still appears and tells
   the judge. A judgment against a slightly wrong name can often be corrected later against the right one.
4. **Calendar** the hearing, the counterclaim date and an action date a week before the hearing.
5. **Place the hold** and tell the approver the same day.
6. **Check insurance** if the claim involves damage or injury.
7. **Check the terms** for a dispute clause. Most consumer arbitration rules let either side take a claim to
   small-claims court, so a clause rarely moves a small claim out. Note what the clause says and leave the decision
   to the approver.

## Never skip the hearing

If the company does not appear, the judge usually hears only the plaintiff and enters a default judgment for the
full amount plus costs. If a date is impossible, ask the court for a new one in writing, as early as possible, in
the way its page describes. If a hearing was missed, there is usually a short window to ask the court to cancel the
judgment (in California, a request filed within 30 days after the clerk mails the notice of judgment); tell the
approver at once.

## Who may appear

- Many courts expect a company to send an owner, officer or regular employee who knows the facts.
- California does not allow lawyers to represent parties at the small-claims hearing. A corporation or other entity
  appears through a regular employee, officer or director who was not hired just to represent it in small claims.
- Some other states allow lawyers, and a few require a lawyer for a corporation or a limited liability company.
  Check the court's page for the state and county on the notice.
- Many courts now offer remote appearance. Request it by the court's deadline if travel is a problem.
- The best representative is the person who knows the record and stays calm, not the most senior one.

## Settle or defend

Work out the numbers on the matter page: the amount claimed, the court's limit, the value of a person's day, and
what the record shows. Then:

- If the customer is right, or mostly right, offer to resolve it before the hearing. A settlement is in writing,
  says it is made without admission, states the amount and the date of payment, and requires the plaintiff to file
  a dismissal with the court. Do not treat the case as over until the court's record shows the dismissal.
- If the customer is wrong and the record is complete, defend.
- A reasonable written offer made before the hearing costs little and shows the judge a reasonable business. Whether
  to mention earlier offers at the hearing is the representative's call; in most courts offers to compromise are
  not evidence of owing anything.

## A claim back against the plaintiff

If the customer owes the company money on the same dealings (an unpaid invoice, damaged equipment), the company can
usually file its own claim in the same case. In California this is the Defendant's Claim form, filed and served at
least 5 days before the hearing, or 1 day before if the company was served 10 days or less before the hearing.
File one only if it is real and documented, and only with the approver's OK. A counterclaim made to intimidate reads
badly.

## The hearing binder

Three identical copies: one for the judge, one for the plaintiff, one for the representative. Check the court's page
for whether exhibits must be sent in or exchanged before the hearing.

1. **Index**, one page.
2. **Timeline**, one page: date, what happened, exhibit number.
3. **The agreement**: the terms or contract the customer accepted, with the proof of acceptance (date, method), and
   the relevant sections marked.
4. **What was promised**: the listing, quote, order or booking as it was.
5. **What was delivered**: delivery or completion records, photos, check-in logs.
6. **Communications** in date order: every message both ways, including the company's offers to fix the problem.
7. **Money**: the charge, any refund or credit, with processor records.
8. **Policies** that applied, as they were on the date.
9. **Statements**: a short signed statement from the person who did the work or handled the customer, saying what
   they did and saw. Bring that person if the court expects live witnesses; many small-claims courts accept written
   statements, and some let the clerk issue a subpoena for a witness or a document.
10. **What the company asks for**: one sentence.

Every page is an original export or a labeled copy. Nothing is edited. Mask other customers' data.

## What to say: the three-minute outline

Judges hear many cases in a session. The representative should be able to say this without reading:

1. Who I am and my role at the company.
2. What the customer ordered and agreed to, and when (exhibit).
3. What the company did, and when (exhibit).
4. What the customer says went wrong, stated fairly.
5. What the record shows about that (two or three exhibits, no more).
6. What the company offered to do about it, and the customer's answer.
7. What the company asks the court to decide.

Conduct: speak to the judge, not to the plaintiff. Do not interrupt. Concede small points that are true. Answer the
question asked. Do not argue law; the judge knows it. Never say anything the binder cannot back.

## After the hearing

- The decision usually comes by mail. Calendar the date it is received.
- **If the company owes money:** pay by the date the judgment gives (in California, generally 30 days), get the
  plaintiff's written acknowledgment that it is paid, and make sure the court's record shows it. Unpaid judgments
  grow with interest and can lead to liens, bank levies and, for licensed trades, trouble with the licence.
- **Appeal:** rules differ sharply. In California only the party who was sued on a claim can appeal it; the appeal
  is a new hearing before a different judge, lawyers are allowed there, and the notice of appeal is due within 30
  days after the clerk mails or hands over the notice of judgment. The approver decides, and a lawyer should weigh in.
- **If the company won:** record it. Do not contact the customer about it.
- Close the matter under step 8 of the skill.

## Limits, for orientation only

Small-claims limits vary from a few thousand dollars to $20,000 or more and change often. California: $12,500 when
the plaintiff is an individual and $6,250 when it is a business. Texas justice courts: $20,000. Several states
raised their limits in 2026. The limit on the notice's own court page is the one that counts. A claim above the
limit belongs in a regular court, where the company needs a lawyer.
