# The interview: invitation, guide, running it

Use it in steps 5 to 7. Examples are invented.

## The invitation

Five sentences. Sent from the team's own address, never from a personal one.

    Subject: 20 minutes on how you send invoices?

    I'm <name>, <a product researcher / an AI teammate> at <company>. You sent <three invoices last week>, and
    I'm trying to understand what that is like for <electricians working alone>.
    Could I ask you a few questions, <by email / on a 20-minute call>? There is nothing to prepare and nothing to buy.
    <For a call: I'd like to record it so I get your words right; only our team sees it.>
    If you'd rather not, ignore this and you won't hear from me again about it.

Rules:

- Say why them, with something true from the record. "You're a valued customer" is not a reason.
- A bot says it is a bot.
- No description of a feature, no "we're thinking of building".
- One reminder at most, after three working days. Then stop.
- Record every invitation and answer on the task, by account id, so nobody is asked twice in 90 days.

## The guide

One page. The questions you need answered (from step 1) go at the top, for you. They are never read to the customer.

### Shape

| Part | Time | What |
|---|---|---|
| Opening | 2 min | Thanks; what this is for; consent to record; "there are no right answers, and I didn't build any of it" |
| Their world | 3 min | What they do, how the work goes on a normal day |
| The last time | 10 min | One real occasion, start to finish |
| Around it | 5 min | What they tried, what they use instead, what it costs |
| Close | 2 min | The two closing questions; what happens with their answers |

For a written interview: the opening as two lines, then three or four questions from "the last time", then one round of follow-ups.

### Questions that work

They ask about something that happened.

- "Tell me about the last time you <did the thing>. When was it?"
- "What were you doing just before?"
- "Then what happened?"
- "What was the hardest part of that?"
- "What did you do about it?"
- "What did you try before this? What made you stop?"
- "What do you use for that today? What does it cost you?" (time, money, a customer)
- "When did you last look for a better way? What did you find?"
- "Who else is involved when this happens?"
- "How do you know when it has gone well?"

Follow-ups, always ready: "What happened next?" "Can you give me an example?" "Why was that?" "How often does that come up? When was the last time?"

### Questions that fail, and what to ask instead

| Do not ask | Why it fails | Ask |
|---|---|---|
| "Would you use a feature that...?" | People are kind about the future | "When did you last need that? What did you do?" |
| "How much would you pay for...?" | A guess, usually generous | "What do you pay today to deal with this?" |
| "Do you think invoicing is a problem?" | Invites agreement | "Walk me through your last invoice." |
| "Was that frustrating?" | Puts the word in their mouth | "How was that?" |
| "What features do you want?" | Produces a wish list with no weight | "What were you trying to get done when you wished for that?" |
| "Do you usually...?" | Produces the ideal self, not the real one | "What did you do last time?" |
| Two questions in one | They answer the easier half | One question, then wait |

### What is not data

- **Compliments.** "It's great, I love it." Ask what they did with it last week.
- **Hypotheticals.** "I would", "I might", "I'd probably".
- **Generalities.** "I always", "I never", "usually". Ask for the last time.
- **Ideas for features.** Note the idea, then ask what they were trying to do. The need is data; the idea is one option.

### What is strong data

- Something they did, with a date.
- Something they paid for, built or cobbled together to cope (a spreadsheet, a second app, a hired helper).
- Something they stopped doing, and why.
- An introduction to someone else with the problem.
- A flat "that's not a problem for me". Write it down as carefully as the rest.

### The never list (print it on the guide)

- Never pitch, demo or describe what might be built.
- Never argue, correct or explain the product. If they got something wrong about it, that is a finding.
- Never promise a feature, a date or a favour.
- Never lead. Never finish their sentence.
- Never ask about anything outside the purpose stated in the invitation.

### Closing

- "What should I have asked that I didn't?"
- "Who else deals with this that I should talk to?"
- "May I come back to you with one or two follow-up questions?"
- Say what happens next, truthfully: "What you told me goes to the people who decide what we work on. I can't tell you what will come of it."

## Recording and consent

Checked 2026-11-18. This is a practical rule, not legal advice.

- **The rule this skill uses everywhere:** tell every person on a call that it is being recorded or transcribed, before it starts, and get a clear yes from each, on the recording. No yes, no recording: take notes, or end the call.
- **United States.** Federal law (18 U.S.C. § 2511) allows a recording when one party to the conversation consents. A minority of states, about a dozen, require every party's consent; California, Florida, Illinois, Pennsylvania and Washington are among those usually listed, and published lists differ on a few states. The rule above satisfies both kinds.
- **Check locally** when a participant is outside the United States. In the EU and the UK a recording is personal data: people must be told what is recorded, why, who sees it and how long it is kept, and can ask for it to be deleted. Other countries have their own rules. When in doubt, ask the owner before recording.
- **Written interviews** also need a line saying how answers are used and kept.
- **Keeping it.** The transcript stays in the tool that made it. Into `knowledge/` go only quotes, the account id and the segment. If a customer asks for their words to be removed, remove the evidence lines and the snapshot, and note the removal (not the content) in the read log.

## After each interview

Within the same working day: the snapshot (format in `evidence-and-findings.md`), the evidence lines, a thank-you of one sentence, and a note on the task of anything they were told would happen (a follow-up question, an introduction).
