# Discovery and qualification with SPICED

SPICED is a qualification framework from Winning by Design. The letters: **S**ituation, **P**ain, **I**mpact,
**C**ritical Event, **D**ecision. It is used here because it starts from the buyer's problem and its consequences
rather than from their budget, and because the same five facts carry straight into the proposal, the mutual action
plan and the handoff. The questions below are original to this library.

If the team chose another method at setup, use that one and ignore this file. Never run two.

## What each letter must establish

| Letter | Met when you can write down | Not met when |
|---|---|---|
| Situation | The facts that decide fit: size, how they do it today, the systems involved, who is affected | They are outside the fit rule in `knowledge/sales.md` |
| Pain | A specific problem, in their words, with a recent example | The problem is vague, borrowed ("we should modernise"), or not one the team solves |
| Impact | What the pain costs: money, time, customers, risk. A number in their units, from them, or a clear statement of consequence from someone who feels it | Nobody there can say what it costs, or the cost is smaller than the price |
| Critical event | A date by which they need it solved, and what happens if they miss it | There is no date and nothing happens if they wait. (Record "none". That is an answer, and usually means nurture.) |
| Decision | Who decides, who must agree, what steps and paperwork a purchase takes, what else they are weighing, and what they will judge by | The person you are talking to cannot find out, or the decision-maker is against it |

"Unknown" is a status of its own. A letter you have assumed is unknown.

## Before the conversation

Fill in what their message, website and any earlier contact already tell you, with the source. Then list only the
gaps. Asking a question they have already answered tells them you did not read what they wrote.

Plan three or four questions you most need answered and let the rest come from what they say.

## Questions, by letter

Pick, do not recite. Ask about what happened, not what they think they would do.

**Situation** (keep this short; most of it you should know already)
- How do you handle <the job> today, from start to finish?
- Who touches it, and what do they use?
- What changed recently that made you look now?

**Pain**
- Tell me about the last time this went wrong. What happened?
- How often does that happen in a normal month?
- What have you already tried? What happened with that?
- If you could fix one part of it and nothing else, which part?

**Impact**
- When it goes wrong, what does it cost you? Walk me through one case.
- Who else feels it: your customers, your team, your boss?
- What happens if nothing changes for another year?
- If this were solved, what would you do with the time or money?
If they have no number, build one with them: how often, times what each one costs, in their own figures. Say it back
and ask if it sounds right. Write down that the inputs were theirs.

**Critical event**
- Is there a date this needs to be working by? What is driving that date?
- What happens if it slips past then?
- Working back from that date, when would you need to decide?
A wish ("as soon as possible") is not a critical event. A renewal of the thing they use now, a busy season, an
audit, a launch, a new hire starting, a budget that expires: those are.

**Decision**
- Besides you, who needs to be comfortable with this before it happens?
- How did the last purchase like this get decided here? How long did it take?
- Is there a security, legal or finance step? Who runs it, and what do they usually ask for?
- What else are you looking at, including carrying on as you are?
- What will you compare us on?
- Is money set aside for this, or does it need to be found?

## How to run the conversation

- **Open by saying what you already know** in two sentences and asking what you got wrong.
- **One question at a time.** Then be quiet and let them finish.
- **Follow the answer.** The best next question is usually "what happened then?" or "why does that matter?"
- **Spread the questions out.** As a guide, one widely read practitioner analysis suggests around 11 to 14
  questions in a discovery call and warns that many more feels like an interrogation, especially to senior people
  (see `sources.md`; it is advice, not a measured rule). In writing, ask at most three per message.
- **Use their words.** Write down the phrases they use for the problem. Those phrases go in the proposal.
- **Do not pitch.** If they ask "can it do X?", answer in one sentence and ask what X would do for them.
- **Tell them when it is not a fit.** As soon as you know.
- **Before the end, recap:** "Here is what I heard", in their words: the problem, what it costs, the date, who
  decides. Ask what you missed.
- **Agree one next step with a date** before the call ends, and who is doing it.

By email or chat the same rules hold, with shorter turns: answer their question, ask one or two of yours, repeat.

## After the conversation

Within the hour:

1. Update the deal record: each letter, status, their words, the date, who said it.
2. Send the recap, under 120 words: what you heard, the next step and its date, anything you promised to find out.
3. Put each promise in `knowledge/follow-ups.md` with a date.
4. Decide: pursue, nurture or disqualify.

## Deciding

- **Pursue** when Pain and Impact are met and no letter is not met. Unknowns are next steps, not blockers, except
  before a proposal.
- **Before a proposal:** Pain, Impact and Decision are met and the Critical Event is known.
- **Nurture** when the fit is real but there is no critical event, no budget path, or the decision-maker is not
  engaged. Agree a date to speak again tied to something on their side, and leave them alone until then.
- **Disqualify** when Situation is outside the fit rule, the pain is not one the team solves, or the impact is
  smaller than the price. Say so plainly and kindly. If you know something that would suit them better, tell them.

A disqualified or nurtured lead is recorded with its reason. It is not a failure; a quarter spent on it would be.

## When the champion is not the decision-maker

Usual, and fine. Do three things:

- ask the champion what the decision-maker cares about and what would make them say no;
- give the champion what they need to make the case inside: the one-page proposal in their numbers;
- ask to meet the decision-maker for a short, specific purpose ("ten minutes to check the numbers are right").

If the champion will not let you near the decision-maker after two asks, mark Decision as unknown and do not
forecast the deal.
