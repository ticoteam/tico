# Willingness to pay: the evidence ladder

Climb from the top. Stop at the first rung that settles the question. Name the rung next to every price you
recommend.

| Rung | What it is | Strength | Use it for |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | What customers already paid | Real money, the company's own customers | Whether the current price has room; where discounts cluster |
| 2 | Conversations about value and price | Reasons, in the buyer's words | New products; which features matter; the range to test |
| 3 | Surveys | Many answers, stated not real | An acceptable range; ranking features |
| 4 | A live test | Real money at the new price | The decision |

## Rung 1: what customers already paid

Read from billing and the CRM, in this run:

- **Realised price against list**, as a distribution (`skills/read-discounts-as-a-distribution`). Nearly everyone at
  list with no pushback suggests room. A cluster at one discount level means that level is the real price.
- **Reaction to past changes.** What happened to conversion, plan mix and churn the last time a price moved.
- **Upgrades and limits.** Customers who pay for more than the plan requires, or who buy add-ons freely, are showing
  what they would pay.
- **Deals won and lost.** Where price was given as the reason, at what number, against which alternative. A win rate
  that barely changes across price levels suggests the price is not what decides.
- **Customers who never ask about price.** In sales-led businesses, no pushback at all is a sign the price is low.

## Rung 2: conversations

Talk to 8 to 15 people per customer group: recent buyers, people who chose something else, and people still deciding.
Do it before a product is built where possible. The aim is reasons and a range, not a statistic.

Ask in this order. Never open with "what would you pay".

1. What were you using before, and what did it cost you in money and time?
2. What would you do if this did not exist?
3. What is it worth to you when it works? Push for their own figure: hours saved, revenue gained, a cost avoided.
4. Of these features, which could you not do without? Which would you never use?
5. At what price would this be an easy yes? At what price would you start to hesitate? At what price would you not
   consider it?
6. Who else has to agree, and what budget does it come from?

Write down the words they use for value. Those words belong on the price page. Never quote a customer in any
output unless the words are theirs and they are on record.

## Rung 3: surveys

Stated answers run high. A review of experiments comparing what people said they would pay with what they then paid
found the stated figure higher in most studies, with a median ratio of about 1.35 (Murphy and others, 2005). Treat a
survey figure as an upper guide, and say so on the page.

| Method | Question it answers | How it works | Limits |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Four price questions (Van Westendorp, 1976) | What range of prices is acceptable? | Each person names the price that is too cheap to trust, a bargain, getting expensive, and too expensive. The overlap gives a range | Gives a range, not the best price; says nothing about how many would buy |
| Stepped price question (Gabor-Granger) | Inside a known range, where does demand drop? | Each person is asked whether they would buy at a price, then a higher or lower one | Needs the range first; people anchor on the first price shown |
| Most and least valuable (MaxDiff) | Which features matter most to which group? | People pick the most and least valuable from short lists, many times | Ranks features; gives no price |
| Trade-off choices (conjoint) | What are features worth, and how do plans compete? | People choose between whole offers with different features and prices | Needs a large sample and expert set-up; only worth it for a big decision |

Rules:

- Survey the group the plan is for, not everyone on the list. Split the results by group.
- Sample size guides from survey vendors (often about 100 completed answers per group) are rules of thumb, not a
  standard. With fewer, report the answers as counts and do not draw a curve.
- Describe the offer the same way the price page will. People price what they are shown.
- A survey never proves a price. It narrows the range to test.

## Rung 4: a live test

See `price-tests.md`. This is the only rung where customers pay the new price with their own money, so it is the one
that decides. The lower rungs choose what to test and keep the test from being a blind guess. The bot designs the
test; it runs only after the owner OKs it.

## Writing the candidate price

For each candidate, write four lines on the page:

1. The price and what it is per.
2. The evidence: rung, numbers, date.
3. What it implies: break-even conversion or break-even revenue churn.
4. What is still unknown, and the test that would settle it.

Where the only evidence is rung 3 or below, the recommendation is "test this range", not "charge this".
