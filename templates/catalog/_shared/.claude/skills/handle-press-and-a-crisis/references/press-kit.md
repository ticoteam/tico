# Press kit: story, release, briefing, pitch

## 1. Is it a story?

A reporter's test is "why would my readers care, and why now?" Check the news against it honestly.

| Test | Passes when |
|---|---|
| New | It happened or will happen at a known time, and has not been reported |
| Matters outside the company | It changes something for customers, a market, a place or a public question |
| Provable | There is data with a source, something to see, or a customer who agreed to talk |
| Has a person | A named person at the company will speak on the record |

Usually a story: original data nobody else has; a first of its kind that can be proven; a named customer's result;
a funding round or acquisition; a clear position on a public question, from someone qualified to hold it.
Usually not: a feature update, a redesign, an award that was applied for, a hire below the top team, an anniversary.
Those go on the company's own channels.

**Size and timing.**

| Size | Example | Press work starts | What is made |
|---|---|---|---|
| Major | New product, funding, a big partnership | Four weeks before | Full kit, release, about five to ten tailored pitches, briefing |
| Smaller | A notable customer, a data report, a local launch | Two weeks before | Fact sheet, about five tailored pitches, briefing |
| Reactive | A reporter is covering the company's field this week | The same day | A short note offering a qualified person and one fact |

## 2. The fact sheet (always)

One page. It is the source for everything else in the kit.

- The news in one sentence.
- Five to ten facts, each with its source and date. Mark each "public" or "cleared for this story".
- The company in two sentences (from the Brand card), founding year, where it is based.
- Who can speak: name, role, when available.
- Customers who agreed to talk, and what each can speak about.
- Links: pictures, product, earlier coverage.
- The press contact and how fast replies come.

Anything not on the fact sheet does not go in a pitch or a release.

## 3. The release (only when warranted)

A release suits news that many outlets will want in the same form: funding, a major launch, a partnership, a formal
statement. For most stories a good pitch and the fact sheet do more.

Under 500 words. The most important thing first, then less important things in order, so it can be cut from the
bottom.

1. **Headline:** the news, plainly, with the company's name. No pun.
2. **Place and date**, then the **first paragraph**: who did what, when, and why it matters. A reader who stops here
   has the story.
3. **Second paragraph:** the proof: the number, the customer, what can be seen.
4. **A quote** from a named person that says something only a person can: why, what they believe, what comes next.
   Not a restatement of the first paragraph.
5. **Details:** how it works, price, availability, dates.
6. **A second quote** only from an outside party (a customer, a partner) with their approval.
7. **About the company:** two sentences.
8. **Contact.**

Rules: every claim passes the claims gate in `write-marketing-copy`. No superlative without a source. No future
dates the product owner has not confirmed. Publishing it on the site or a wire needs an OK.

## 4. Quotes

- A quote is something a person said or wrote, or wording drafted for them that they then approved as theirs.
- Record the approval: who, when, which version.
- A customer's quote also needs their company's permission where their name or employer appears.
- A good quote has an opinion in it. "We are thrilled to announce" is not one.

## 5. The spokesperson briefing

One page, sent before any interview.

- **The reporter:** outlet, beat, two recent pieces with dates, the angle they seem to be taking.
- **The format:** call or email, length, recorded or not, deadline. Assume everything is on the record.
- **Three points to make**, each with its proof.
- **Hard questions**, at least five, each with an honest short answer. Include the one the company would least like.
- **What not to share**, from `knowledge/rules.md`, and what to say instead ("We have not announced that").
- **Do not guess.** "I don't know. I'll find out and send it today" is a good answer, and then send it.
- **Do not speak for others**: competitors, customers, regulators.
- **Afterwards:** what was promised to the reporter, by when.

## 6. The pitch

One reporter, one email, written for them.

| Part | Rule |
|---|---|
| Subject | Under eight words. The news, not "Story idea" or "Quick question". |
| First line | Their own piece, with its date, and why this follows from it. Shows you read it. No flattery. |
| The news | Two sentences. What is new and why their readers care. |
| The proof | One number with its source, or the customer who will talk |
| The offer | Who can speak and when. What you can send. |
| The ask | One, with a time: "Would a 15-minute call on Thursday be useful?" |
| Length | Under 150 words in all. Count them. |
| Attachments | None on a first pitch. Link to the kit. |
| Words to avoid | "Exclusive" (unless agreed and true), "revolutionary", "excited to share", "circling back", "per my last email" |

- Send each pitch on its own, to the reporter's public work address, at a reasonable hour in their time zone.
- The reason must be specific to that reporter. If the first line would work for any reporter, they are the wrong
  reporter or you have not read enough.
- Do not pitch two reporters at the same outlet at once without telling each.
- Do not send text that reads as machine-made. Reporters filter it. Run it past `write-marketing-copy`'s AI-tell
  list.

**The follow-up.** One, after three working days. Two or three sentences with something new. Reply in the same
thread. After that, stop: do not call, do not message their personal accounts.

**What to expect.** Most pitches get no reply. Industry surveys of reporters, as recorded in Tico's PR notes in
2026, say the chief complaint is pitches off their beat, that most prefer one-to-one email, and that reply rates
run to a few in a hundred. Judge the list by replies and coverage per pitch, and improve the fit, not the volume.

## 7. Embargoes and exclusives

- **Embargo:** offer it only if the owner agreed. Ask first, with no details: "I have news on [topic] under embargo
  until [date and time, with time zone]. Would you like it on that basis?" Send details only after a written yes.
  An embargo a reporter never agreed to does not bind them.
- **Exclusive:** one outlet, agreed by the owner, with a deadline for the reporter to decide. While it is out, the
  story goes to no one else. If they pass, say so before offering it elsewhere.
- Both are promises the company must keep. Breaking one costs the relationship.

## 8. When a reporter writes to you

1. Reply the same day, even only to say when a full answer will come. Ask their deadline and what they need.
2. If public material answers it, answer. If not, it is an ask to the owner with the question, the deadline and a
   suggested reply.
3. If it is about a problem, a rumour or a person, treat it as level 1 in the crisis playbook, at least.
4. Log it in `knowledge/media-list.md`.

## 9. Coverage

- Log every mention in `knowledge/coverage.md` the day it appears: date, outlet, reporter, angle, tone, pitched or
  not, link.
- Thank the reporter briefly. Do not ask for changes to tone or headline.
- A factual error: one polite note with the correct fact and its evidence.
- Measure by what the company can count: referred visits and sign-ups from its own records, pieces in outlets its
  buyers read, and whether assistants and search results begin citing the piece (`get-found-and-cited`). Do not
  report "advertising value" or "reach" figures: they are estimates with nothing behind them.
