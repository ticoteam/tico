# Required facts, known defenses and traps, by claim type

Use with step 3 and gate 2. For each type: fill every **required fact** from the records, with its source. A missing
required fact blocks the letter. Then write one line on each **defense** the recipient is likely to raise, and read the
**traps**. United States defaults; the "check locally" line says what changes by state.

Nothing here is legal advice. It is the checklist a careful person runs before a lawyer is needed.

## Trademark

Required facts
- The mark, exactly as registered or used, and the goods or services it covers.
- Registration number, register status today, owner of record. Or, for an unregistered mark: the date of first use
  in commerce, where it has been used, and proof (dated invoices, ads, press, the website's archive).
- The recipient's use: the name or logo they use, on what goods or services, where, and the earliest date found.
- Who used first, in the places and for the goods that overlap. If the recipient may have used first, stop: the
  company could be the infringer.
- Why buyers would be confused: similar names, similar goods, same channels, same customers. Any real confusion
  (a misdirected email, a review on the wrong page) with its date.

Defenses to consider
- The recipient used the name first, or in a region the company had not reached.
- The goods or services are unrelated.
- The term is descriptive or generic for the goods.
- Fair use: the recipient uses the word to describe its own goods, or names the company's product to refer to it
  truthfully (resale, comparison, compatibility, commentary).
- Delay: the company knew for a long time and did nothing.
- The registration is vulnerable (not used for three years or more, or wrongly owned).

Traps
- The symbol for a registered mark may be used only for a mark registered with the national office.
- A registration covers its listed goods and services. Do not write as if it covers everything.
- Resale of genuine goods is generally lawful. A letter that treats a reseller as a counterfeiter is an overclaim.
- A letter that says "you are infringing" to a funded recipient is the classic trigger for a suit in their home court.
  Courts look at all the circumstances, and a single letter can be enough.

Check locally: state unfair-competition claims and state registrations, only if the letter relies on them.

## Copyright

Required facts
- The work: what it is, who made it, when, and how the company owns it (employee work, or a written assignment).
- Registration number and date, if registered. If not registered, say so on the matter page.
- The copy: where it appears, since when, and a side-by-side showing what was taken.
- That the recipient had access (the work was public, or they received it).

Defenses to consider
- Fair use: commentary, criticism, news, teaching, parody, or a small amount that does not replace the original.
- The recipient has a licence (check the company's own terms, stock-library terms and any open licence).
- The material copied is facts, ideas, a common layout or a short phrase, none of which copyright protects.
- Independent creation.

Traps
- A US work generally must be registered before a suit can be filed. Statutory damages and attorney's fees are
  generally available only if registration came before the infringement began, or within three months of first
  publication. Do not mention statutory damages or fees unless the registration date supports it.
- A DMCA takedown notice to a host or platform is a statement under penalty of perjury that the sender is authorized
  to act for the owner. Fair use must be considered before sending one, and a knowingly false notice creates
  liability. The signer makes that statement, with an OK.
- The contractor who made the work owns it unless a signed writing assigns it. Check before asserting ownership.

Check locally: nothing; copyright is federal. For a recipient abroad, stop (hard stop).

## Defamation

Required facts
- The exact words, quoted in full, with where and when they were published and by whom.
- That they are about the company or a named person at it.
- That each statement complained of is a statement of fact, and is false. For each one, the document that proves
  it false.
- Who saw it, and the harm, from the record (a lost order with its date, a cancelled contract). Not guessed.
- The date the team first saw it. Limitation periods are short: often one year from publication, up to three in
  some states.

Defenses to consider
- Truth, or substantial truth. If the gist is right, there is no claim.
- Opinion, rhetoric or obvious exaggeration ("the worst service I've had").
- Privilege: statements in court papers, to regulators or the police, and fair reports of them.
- For a business that is in the public eye, the speaker may need to have known the statement was false or been
  reckless about it.
- The website or platform hosting someone else's words is generally immune under federal law (section 230 of the
  Communications Decency Act). The claim, if any, is against the person who wrote it.

Traps
- Around forty states and the District of Columbia have anti-SLAPP laws (count read November 2026; it changes most
  years). A suit over speech on a public issue, which often includes consumer reviews, can be struck early and the
  company ordered to pay the speaker's legal fees. A letter that threatens such a suit is weak, and it gets published.
- The Consumer Review Fairness Act bars form contract terms that forbid or penalize honest customer reviews. Never
  cite a non-disparagement clause in the company's customer terms against a reviewer.
- Some states require a written demand for retraction or correction within a set time before certain damages can be
  claimed, and say what it must contain. If the state has such a law, the letter is drafted to meet it.
- The letter repeats the statement to no one but the speaker. Sending it to their employer or followers is a new
  publication by the company.
- A calm factual public reply or a platform policy report usually works better than a letter. Recommend those first
  for a customer or a reviewer (`write-a-public-review-reply`, `judge-a-review-flag` where the team has them).

Check locally: the limitation period, the anti-SLAPP statute and its fee rule, and any retraction statute, for the
speaker's state and the company's state. Write each with its source and date.

## Contract breach

Required facts
- The signed agreement: parties (exact legal names), date, the version that is in force, and any amendments.
- The clause breached, quoted with its section number from the signed text.
- The breach: what was done or not done, on what dates, with the document that shows each.
- That the company has done its own part, or why its performance was excused. If the company is itself behind on
  an obligation, tell the approver before drafting.
- The notice clause: who must be notified, at what address, by what method, and when notice counts as given.
- The cure clause: how long the other side has to fix it, and what happens after.
- What the company wants: performance, cure, payment of a stated sum (with the arithmetic), or to end the contract.

Defenses to consider
- The company breached first, or waived the term by accepting the same conduct before without objection.
- The clause is ambiguous, or was changed by a later email or course of dealing.
- A limitation-of-liability, exclusive-remedy or dispute-resolution clause narrows what can be demanded.
- The other side has its own claim it will raise in reply.

Traps
- A notice that does not follow the notice clause (wrong address, email where the clause requires courier) may not
  count. Follow it exactly, and send a courtesy copy by email as well.
- Decide which letter this is: a notice of breach with a chance to cure, or a termination. Do not write words like
  "this terminates" or "we treat the agreement as ended" unless the approver has decided to terminate and the
  contract's own conditions for it are met. A wrongful termination makes the company the party in breach.
- Keep performing the company's own obligations unless the approver decides otherwise, and say that performance
  continues "under a full reservation of rights".
- If the contract requires negotiation, mediation or arbitration before any other step, the letter starts that
  process in the way the clause says.
- A demand for payment sent to an individual consumer is a different activity with its own rules. Use the team's
  receivables skill, not this one.

Check locally: the governing-law clause names the state. Check that state's limitation period for written contracts
and any rule on notice the letter relies on.

## Non-solicit (customers, staff) and other post-relationship promises

Required facts
- The signed agreement with the restriction, quoted with its section number, and the date it was signed.
- Who is bound, and in what role (seller of a business, former contractor, former partner, former employee).
- The restriction's length, what it covers and where. That it has not expired.
- The state where the person lives and works, and the governing-law clause.
- The conduct: who was approached, by whom, when and how, with the document for each (an email forwarded by a
  customer, a message, a public post). A customer simply moving is not proof of solicitation.
- What confidential material, if any, left with them, with proof. Without proof, do not allege it.

Defenses to consider
- The restriction is void or narrowed by state law.
- The customer or teammate made the first approach.
- The restriction is broader than needed to protect a real business interest.
- The contact list was public or the person's own.

Traps
- California voids non-competes and most staff and customer non-solicits for workers, wherever the contract was
  signed, and makes it unlawful to try to enforce one (hard stop). Several other states restrict them by pay level,
  job type or notice rules, and the list changes every year.
- The federal ban on non-competes never took effect: a court set the rule aside in 2024, the agency dropped its
  appeal in September 2025 and removed the rule in February 2026. State law governs, and the agency still brings
  individual cases.
- A letter to the person's new employer or to customers can bring a claim for interfering with their business.
  Only a lawyer sends those (hard stop).
- Restrictions tied to the sale of a business are treated more kindly than those tied to a job. Say which this is.
- A claim that trade secrets were taken, against a former teammate, goes to a lawyer (hard stop).

Check locally: always. Name the state, read its current statute or an official summary this month, and record the
source and date. If it was not checked, the letter is not sent.
