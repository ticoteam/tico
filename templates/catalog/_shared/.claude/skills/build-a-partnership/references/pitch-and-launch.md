# Pitch, first conversation and launch

All sample wording is invented. Write fresh words for the partner in front of you.

## The pitch

The reader is busy, is pitched "partnerships" every week, and is measured on their own numbers. They will give the
message a few seconds. It must show, fast, that this is specific to them and that the first step is small.

Shape, up to 120 words:

1. **The evidence of overlap,** as a number from the team's records. "Eleven of our customers use your product."
2. **What they gain,** in their terms. Revenue, keeping their customers, a gap filled, something their customers
   keep asking them for.
3. **One small first step.** One shared customer to help together, one account to compare notes on, a 20-minute
   call. Two real times if you offer a call.
4. **Sign-off** as the team's AI teammate.

Leave out: the word "synergy" and its relatives; the team's history; a program with tiers; a deck; a contract; any
ask for exclusivity or commitment; any customer's name without that customer's consent.

Where to send it, best first: an introduction from a shared customer who is happy to make it; a teammate's contact;
the person who owns partnerships there; the product owner for the relevant area. Use
`find-a-route-to-a-partner-target`. A cold message to a general address is the last resort.

If the candidate is the one who wrote first, reply inside the team's reply time, answer what they asked, and run
the fit scorecard before agreeing to anything. An inbound proposal is flattering and still needs to score.

Follow-up: at most two more messages, a week apart, each adding something (a new overlap figure, a customer's
request, a simpler first step). Then stop and rescore next quarter.

## The first conversation

It is discovery. Aim to leave knowing:

- what they and their team are measured on this year;
- which of their customers this would help, and how many;
- how they work with partners now, what has worked and what went wrong before;
- who would do the work on their side, and how much time that person really has;
- what they would need from the team to say yes to a first step;
- anything in their own agreements that limits what they can do.

Say plainly what the team can and cannot do. Do not promise engineering work, a fee, or a launch date in this
conversation. Agree one first step with a date and an owner on each side. Send the recap within the hour.

## Proving one win

Before any agreement, do one real thing together within 30 days:

| Type | A good first win |
|---|---|
| Referral | They introduce one prospect; the team follows up within the reply time and reports back what happened |
| Service partner | One customer set up jointly, with the partner doing the work and the team supporting |
| Integration | The connection working for one shared customer, even if rough, with their feedback |
| Co-marketing | One small shared piece (a guide, a webinar, a newsletter swap) with leads counted on both sides |
| Reseller | One joint sale where the team and the reseller's seller work the deal together |

Keep a dated record of who did what and what the customer got. That record becomes the basis for the term sheet,
the enablement kit and the partner's own internal case for doing more.

If the first win needs money to change hands, customer data to be shared, or engineering time, it needs the
matching OK first.

## The enablement kit

What a partner's people need so they can act without asking. Short, and theirs to keep:

1. **One page: who it is for and who it is not.** The team's fit rule, in plain words, with two examples of a good
   referral and one of a poor one.
2. **How to spot the need:** three things a customer says that mean they should hear about the product.
3. **How to describe it** in two sentences, and the three questions customers ask most, with answers.
4. **How to pass a lead or register a deal,** in one step, and what happens next, with times.
5. **What they get and when:** the fee, the payment timing, where to see status.
6. **Who to contact,** and how fast they will hear back.
7. **For service and reseller partners:** a way to try the product themselves, a short walkthrough to give, and the
   setup checklist.
8. **For integration partners:** the documentation, a test account, known limits, and who to tell about a problem.
9. **Rules:** what they may say and not say about the product (no promises the product does not keep), how the
   names and logos may be used, and the duty to tell prospects when they are paid for a recommendation.

Check every claim in the kit against its source before it goes out. A partner repeating a wrong claim is the team's
problem.

## The 30-60-90 launch

Write it as a table in the partner's file, with an owner and date on every row.

**Days 1 to 30: set up and first activity**
- People named on both sides: who owns the relationship, who sells or refers, who fixes problems.
- Access given: partner account, tracking link or registration route, test account.
- Kit delivered and walked through with the people who will use it.
- Rules of engagement shared in writing.
- First registered lead, first joint customer, or first working integration.
- A rhythm agreed (default: a short written update every two weeks).

**Days 31 to 60: able without help**
- Their people can describe and show the product on their own. Check by asking one to do it.
- First real pipeline: leads registered and being worked, each with a status the partner can see.
- First problems found and fixed: a confusing step, a slow reply, a missing answer added to the kit.
- For an integration: used by more than the first customer; errors monitored; support knows how to help.

**Days 61 to 90: result or reason**
- First closed customer through the partner, or a clear written reason there is none yet.
- The first fee paid on time, if one is due.
- The first joint review held (see `partner-scorecard-and-review.md`).
- A decision: continue as is, change something specific, or stop. All three are acceptable outcomes.

One practitioner rule of thumb read for this skill puts a healthy program at 40 to 60 percent of new partners
registering a deal in their first 90 days. It comes from a vendor with no stated sample, so treat it as a rough
marker; the team's own rate after a year is the better guide.

## Launching an integration

Do not announce until all four are true:

1. Documentation for customers is live and has been followed by someone who did not write it.
2. Both companies' support and sales people have been briefed and know who to send problems to.
3. Monitoring is in place: you would know within a day if it broke.
4. Both sides have agreed the announcement date and what each will publish.

Then: tell the customers who asked for it first, by name, before the general announcement. List it wherever each
side lists integrations. Record how many customers connect in the first 30 and 90 days, and what happens to those
customers' retention compared with before, once there are enough to tell.

A public announcement that names the partner needs the owner's OK and the partner's written agreement on wording.

## When a partner goes quiet

- At 30 days of silence: send something useful (a lead for them, a customer story, a fix) and one question.
- At 60: ask the owner of the relationship directly whether the timing is wrong.
- At 90 with no activity: propose one specific dated step. If it does not happen, mark them lapsed, tell them
  warmly that the door is open, stop the updates, and free the slot.

A lapsed partner is not a failed one. Many come back when their priorities change, if the ending was graceful.
