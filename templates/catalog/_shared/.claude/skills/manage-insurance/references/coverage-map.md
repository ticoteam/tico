# The coverage map

Start from what the company does, not from a list of products. For each line: does the trigger apply, which policy
answers it, and what to check in that policy. A company run mostly by bots with a few people still has customers,
data, contracts and sometimes hardware, so most lines apply; workers' compensation and employment practices shrink
with headcount but do not vanish at one employee.

## Two ways a liability policy is triggered

- **Occurrence.** The policy in force when the harm happened pays, even if the claim arrives years later. Usual for
  general liability. Keep every old occurrence policy forever: it may still be the one that responds.
- **Claims-made.** The policy in force when the claim is made pays, and only for acts after its **retroactive date**.
  Usual for professional liability, cyber, D&O and employment practices. Three rules follow:
  1. Never let it lapse. A gap of a day can leave years of past work uninsured.
  2. When changing carrier, the new policy keeps the old retroactive date in writing, and anything already known is
     reported to the old carrier before it expires.
  3. When cancelling, or when the company is sold or closed, price a **tail** (extended reporting period), which lets
     claims be reported after the policy ends.

(Definitions follow the Insurance Information Institute's small business guide, read 2026-11-18.)

## The map

| If the company... | It needs | Check in the policy |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Has customers, visitors, premises or advertising | **General liability**: injury to people, damage to their property, advertising injury. Often sold with property cover as a business owner's policy | It does not cover mistakes in the company's work or advice, data events, or employees' injuries. Whether products are included |
| Owns or leases equipment, stock or space | **Property**, and business interruption | Whether goods away from the premises (a warehouse, a 3PL, in transit) are included; valuation; what the lease makes the company insure |
| Sells software, services or advice | **Professional liability (errors and omissions; technology E&O for software)**: a customer's financial loss from a mistake, a failure to deliver, or bad output | Claims-made. The description of covered services matches what is actually sold. Ask the broker in writing whether work done by AI agents is covered or excluded, and file the answer |
| Holds personal or customer data, takes payments, or depends on its systems | **Cyber**: its own costs (legal help, forensics, restoring data, notifying people, lost income, extortion) and claims by others (lawsuits, regulators) | See the cyber checklist below |
| Has even one employee | **Workers' compensation and employer's liability** | See the workers' compensation notes below |
| Has employees, or interviews candidates | **Employment practices liability**: discrimination, harassment, wrongful termination claims | Claims-made. Whether contractors' claims are included |
| Has outside investors, a board, or plans to raise money | **Directors and officers**: claims that leaders' decisions caused financial harm. It covers wrongful acts, not injury or property damage | Claims-made. Who is an insured person. Investors often require it in the financing documents: read them |
| Makes, imports, brands or sells a physical product | **Product liability** (injury or damage from the product, often the "products and completed operations" part of general liability), **product recall** (the cost of getting products back; usually a separate policy), and cover for **stock in storage and transit** | That products are not excluded from general liability; territory (where sold and where made); whether the manufacturer's policy names the company as additional insured; recall is not in a standard liability policy |
| Has anyone drive for work, even in their own or a rented car | **Hired and non-owned auto**; commercial auto if it owns vehicles | Often added to general liability by endorsement |
| Moves money on instruction, or could be tricked into paying a fraudster | **Crime**, including social engineering fraud | Often a small sub-limit inside cyber or crime policies; read the figure |
| Has a contract requiring higher limits than its policies carry | **Umbrella or excess** | An umbrella generally sits over general liability, auto and employer's liability, and does not cover professional liability, employment practices, recall or workers' compensation |
| Depends on one or two people | Key person life or disability cover | The owner's choice; list it as "not needed" or "owner to decide" |

Limits are set from three things, in order: the highest limit any contract, lease or lender requires; the loss the
company could not survive; and the broker's view of what similar companies carry. Do not pick a limit from memory or
from a web article.

## Workers' compensation notes

Read on the Insurance Information Institute's workers' compensation page and the SBA's hiring guide, 2026-11-18.

- Each state sets its own rules. Texas is the only state where private employers may generally go without; some
  states exempt very small employers (the guide cites thresholds of three to five employees). Read the state
  agency's page for each work state.
- A policy covers only the states listed on its declarations page. Tell the carrier before an employee starts
  working in a new state.
- A few states sell this cover only through a state fund, and "other states" wording cannot extend to them. From
  general knowledge these are North Dakota, Ohio, Washington and Wyoming; confirm on each state's page.
- In most states an uninsured contractor or subcontractor can be treated as the company's employee for this
  purpose. This is why certificates are collected from contractors.
- The premium is set from payroll by job class, then audited after the term. Give real payroll figures from the
  provider, by state and class.
- Owners and officers can sometimes be excluded or included by choice; the state decides. Ask the broker.

## Cyber checklist

From the FTC's cyber insurance guidance for small businesses, developed with the NAIC (undated; read 2026-11-18).
Ask whether the policy:

- Covers breaches of data the company holds and data held for it by vendors and cloud providers.
- Covers attacks on the network, anywhere in the world, including ones called terrorism.
- Gives a duty to defend, so the carrier provides the lawyer in a lawsuit or regulatory investigation.
- Pays in excess of other insurance that applies.
- Has a breach hotline open every day and hour.
- Pays first-party costs: legal counsel, restoring data, notifying customers, lost income, crisis communications,
  extortion and fraud, forensics, fines and penalties.
- Pays third-party costs: payments to affected people, claims and settlements, defamation and intellectual
  property claims, litigation and responding to regulators.

Also read: the security controls the application says the company has (they must be true on the day of a claim),
any requirement to use the carrier's own lawyers and forensic firms, and the sub-limits for ransomware and fraud.
