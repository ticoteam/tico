# OKR practice: writing and grading

How this bot writes and grades objectives and key results. The sources disagree on scales and cut-offs, so the team's
own rules belong in `knowledge/rhythm.md`. This file gives the mechanics and the conventions the bot uses until then.

## Writing

- An objective is an outcome in plain words. A key result is a measure with a baseline, a target and a date. An
  activity ("run three webinars") is not a key result; move it under "how we might get there".
- Keep the set small: three to five objectives, about three key results each. That is the default cap, and the team's
  number in `rhythm.md` replaces it.

## Grading on 0 to 1

- A key result's grade is the fraction of the way from its baseline to its target, read from the final dated reading.
  A result that reaches or passes its target is 1.0. When the target is below the baseline, the fraction is worked out the same
  way and then clamped to 0 to 1 (`skills/grade-a-key-result-from-readings/SKILL.md`). A binary result is 0 or 1.
- A result with no reading is "ungraded", never 0 and never 1. Say what was missing.
- The total averages graded results only, and says how many were left out.
- Google's public OKR guidance grades from 0.0 to 1.0, as one summary of its ReWork guide describes it. A Tability article
  argues teams should not copy Google's scoring exactly. The excerpts read for this file did not cover its 0.6 to 0.7 point.

## Committed and aspirational

- A committed result is expected to reach 1.0. A committed result below 1.0 needs one sentence on why.
- An aspirational result is expected to land around 0.6 to 0.7. Third-party guides present this convention; it is not
  a rule from the framework's authors as read here.
- A total of 1.0 everywhere suggests the targets were too easy. A total under 0.4 suggests they were out of reach or the
  plan was wrong.
- Colour bands for grades vary. A reader's notes page on one OKR book lists green at 0.7 to 1.0, yellow at 0.4 to 0.6 and
  red at 0.0 to 0.3. That is a low-authority example of variation, not a standard.

## Limits

- Sources are vendor and consultancy pages, and search results read on 2026-11-01, not full pages. The 0.6 to 0.7 figure
  comes from third-party guides. The book that popularised OKRs was not read for this file, so do not cite it as read.
